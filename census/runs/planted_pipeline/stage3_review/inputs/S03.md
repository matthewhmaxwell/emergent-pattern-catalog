**Full text:**

**Verdict: KNOWN.** What you observed is a documented behaviour: cells form strong aggregates, and those aggregates keep moving or rearranging because the cells also climb the gradient of a nutrient they consume. I'm overturning the first pass's "variant" call. Nothing in the phenomenology goes beyond what's already described.

**Why the two features look like a known mechanism:**
- **Aggregation (f_std ≈ 2).** Drift up the attractant the cells make, with saturating sensitivity b/(1+b²), is a standard Keller–Segel auto-attractant set-up. Patterns form when the chemical signal acts as an auto-attractant, that is, when cells both produce and migrate up gradients of it. Your attractant-only sub-run fits this: aggregates form (f_std 1.64) but are almost frozen (f_change 0.007).
- **Persistent motion (f_change ≈ 0.29) needs the nutrient term.** Since cells eat the nutrient, it is lowest where cells pile up. Moving up its gradient therefore pushes cells away from crowds, so the nutrient acts like a self-made repellent. Rough linearised estimate: its range is about √(0.75/0.084) ≈ 3, versus √2.5 ≈ 1.6 for the attractant, and it recovers slowly. That is short-range attraction plus longer-range, slower repulsion: the attraction–repulsion Keller–Segel set-up. For that system, Liu, Shi & Wang (2013) showed that chemotactic competitive interaction between attraction and repulsion can produce periodic patterns which are impossible for the chemotaxis model with a single chemical. They also found time-periodic rippling wave patterns and spiky steady state patterns.
- **The same set-up has been simulated directly.** A PLoS ONE (2013) study modelled bacteria that chemotactically sense gradients in either ambient or self-secreted attractants (e.g. nutrient or aspartate respectively), or in both. It found that when nutrient sensitivity is high enough relative to the secreted attractant, the bacterial spots become motile and the eventual pattern is a ring made up or merging moving spots rather than an amorphous or regular array of static ones. That is your attractant-only versus full-program contrast. Their model also includes replication, which yours doesn't, but the mechanism behind the moving spots is the same.
- **Related dynamics.** Ongoing merging and re-emergence of aggregates is also documented in chemotaxis models with growth (Painter & Hillen 2011). There, patterns evolve through merging and emerging. Here the nutrient term, rather than growth, stops the aggregates simply coarsening and freezing.
- **The model family is also known.** Cells, a self-made attractant and a consumed nutrient are the ingredients of the Tyson–Lubkin–Murray models of E. coli and Salmonella patterns (bacteria, nutrient and chemoattractant are modeled mathematically and give rise to a nonlinear partial differential equation system).

**Genuine pattern, not an artefact:**
- Both features are zero when interactions are knocked out, and they hold up at 4x area and 3x run length.
- Neither feature on its own needs both rules, but neither sub-program comes close to the full program on both together. That fits a real interaction: attraction makes the aggregates, and the nutrient term keeps them moving.
- The saturating sensitivity bounds the drift, so the run blowing up numerically is unlikely.
- f_change is large and stays large over the longer run, so this is not a frozen state.

**Caveat:** f_std and f_change can't tell apart moving spots, regular oscillation and irregular merging and re-emergence. All three are documented, so the verdict stands either way.
